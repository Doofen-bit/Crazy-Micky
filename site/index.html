NO BIOLOGY BACKGROUND NEEDED

The experiment in 30 seconds

  1. 1 Stress

    For 15 days, each test mouse was briefly attacked by a larger, aggressive mouse, then lived next to it behind a see-through divider (chronic social defeat). Control mice were never attacked.

  2. 2 Sort

    A social test placed each stressed mouse in an arena with an unfamiliar mouse behind mesh. Resilient mice still approached it. Susceptible mice avoided it and hid in the corners, a depression-like behaviour.

  3. 3 Sample

    Brains were collected at four times of day, two in the lights-on rest phase and two in the lights-off active phase (mice are night-active). Two regions were taken: the SCN, the brain's master clock, and the habenula (Hb), which processes bad experiences.

  4. 4 Measure

    RNA-seq reads the RNA messages in each sample and counts how many came from each gene. That tells us how active each of ~46,000 genes was. We then compared the groups one gene at a time.

Our question: which genes differ between resilient, susceptible and never-stressed mice, and does the answer depend on the brain region and the time of day?

The original study (Narain et al., 2024) reported that most differences appeared at night (ZT16) and in resilient mice. This site lets you check that claim yourself in our own re-analysis of their public data.

Read the evidence carefully: what these charts can and cannot tell you

This is exploration, not proof. We re-analysed the authors' public data with a standard tool (DESeq2). Each box in View 1 is a separate comparison inside one brain region and one time of day. A positive log₂ fold change means the gene is more active in the first group named. Changing the cutoffs only re-sorts stored results; it does not re-run any statistics.

Missing is not zero. Some genes have too few reads for a reliable test. They have no adjusted p-value, so they are left out of View 2's vertical axis, but you can still search for them and see their activity in View 3.

Limits. Only male mice were used, with 3–5 mice per group. Each sample is a piece of brain tissue containing many cell types, and everything comes from one experiment. A gene that is significant at one time but not another does not prove a formal time effect. The two regions come from the same mice, but we do not test differences between regions.

THE TAKEAWAY

Same stress. Different brains.
And the clock matters.

–

Bouncing back from stress may not simply mean “nothing changed”. In these mice, resilience comes with its own brain activity, and when you look matters as much as where.

One study, male mice only, 3–5 mice per group. These are patterns worth testing further, not a diagnosis.
